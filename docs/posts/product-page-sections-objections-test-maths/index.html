A Product Page for a Digital Download: Eight Sections, Eighteen Objections and the Test Maths

A listing with views and no sales is usually answering the wrong question, in the wrong order. Eight sections in the order a buyer asks them, the objections that block a $19 download, and the sample size a test needs before it can tell you anything.

The studio's own Gumroad shop drew 42 views in thirty days and sold nothing. That is not a verdict on the writing. It is a verdict on testing: at 42 views, no page test can report anything back. What a page can do at any traffic is answer the questions a buyer is already asking, in the order they ask them.

A digital download has no shelf and no salesperson. The page is the whole shop floor, and every sentence either closes a doubt or opens one.

Views without sales is a diagnosis, not a mystery

Get two numbers first: views, and sales per view. Then be honest about what they can tell you. With zero sales in 42 views, the 95% confidence range for the true conversion rate still runs from 0% to about 8% — the page may be fine and simply unseen. Below roughly a hundred views there is no signal, only a reason to fix what is obviously missing.

That leaves one useful question: which doubt is the page not answering? Buyers do not read top to bottom. They arrive with a specific worry — "is this for someone like me", "what is in the file", "is this a subscription" — and they leave when the page does not settle it. The sections are therefore not a narrative but a queue of answers.

The eight sections, in the order doubts arrive

SectionDoubt it answersWhat goes in itLength
1. Outcome lineWhat is this, and does it fix my problem?One sentence naming the artefact and the job25 words
2. InventoryWhat arrives after I pay?Counts, file names, formats, page counts, tool compatibilityA table

The order is not decoration. Section 3 precedes proof because a buyer who is not the audience will not care how good the file is. Section 6 follows the inventory because a price read before the contents is just a number.

Write the inventory before the copy

Sellers describe a product in adjectives because adjectives are easier than counting. Buyers price-check with arithmetic: not "packed with value" but "45 section patterns, 18 objection scripts, a 40-point audit and 20 test briefs, as Markdown and JSON, plus a 14-page PDF and one HTML page that runs offline". That one sentence says how much there is, what format it arrives in, whether their tools can open it, and whether they are buying a document or a working tool.

  • Count things. Files, patterns, prompts, pages — a number is a promise that can be checked.
  • Name the format. PDF, DOCX, Notion, CSV, JSON, a single HTML file. Format decides whether the buyer can use it at all.
  • State the boundary. "Written for solo products between $9 and $49; not for SaaS pricing" costs one line and pre-empts the worst refund request.

Eighteen objections, answered in one line each

Every digital product has a fixed set of objections. A page that ignores them does not remove them; it leaves the buyer to resolve them alone, which usually means leaving.

The buyer thinksA bad page saysWhat closes it
Will it work with my tools?"Works with everything!""Markdown, JSON and one HTML file. No account, no install."
Is this just a pile of AI prompts?"AI-powered!""It is a decision list: each pattern carries the rule for when to use it."
Will it be out of date in a month?"Always updated!""Version-dated inside the file, with a changelog."
What if it does not fit my niche?"Fits any business""Built for solo digital products; enterprise pricing is out of scope."
Is this a subscription?"No hidden fees!""One payment. No account, no renewal."
Can I use it for client work?"Sure!""Personal and client projects, yes. Reselling the files, no."
How long until this does anything?"Instantly!""One evening to rewrite the page, then two to four weeks of traffic."

Two of those answers carry the most weight: format decides whether the buyer can use the thing, and the boundary makes everything else believable.

Price belongs under the inventory

Place the price after the contents, never before, and put the comparison beside it: one freelancer hour, one month of a subscription tool, or the afternoon it would take to build the page yourself. A permanent "was $29, now $19" teaches every visitor to wait for the next discount and makes the base price look invented. Discount codes are for deadlines, not for the everyday page.

The test maths before the test

A page test is a two-proportion test. At 95% confidence and 80% power, the visitors needed per variant are:

n ≈ 7.84 × (p₁(1−p₁) + p₂(1−p₂)) ÷ (p₂ − p₁)²

BaselineTargetVisitors per variant
1%2%2,310
2%3%3,820
2%2.5%13,790
3%4%5,290
5%6%8,150
5%7%2,210

Read rows two and three together, because they are the trap. Moving 2% to 3% is a 50% relative lift and needs about 3,800 visitors per side. Moving 2% to 2.5% is a 25% lift and needs about 13,800 — nearly four times the traffic for a smaller change. The maths sees relative lift, not percentage points.

Put your own traffic in: at 42 views a month, that test needs 7,640 views across both variants — fifteen years. That is not a reason to give up on the page; it is a reason to stop calling hunches tests.

  • Fix the first screen first. The outcome line and the inventory do more than everything else combined, and need no measurement to justify.
  • Test one thing, and only with traffic. Hero line, price placement or the "who it is for" line — one at a time, four figures of views.
  • Do not test taste. Button colour, font and paragraph length below the fold are not worth the traffic.
  • When traffic is small, watch people. Five buyers reading the page aloud will find the broken sentence that a quarter of a million visitors would have been needed to prove.

If you want to see how rival listings change over a launch — price edits, new sections, a rewritten first line — a watcher such as Browse AI returns those changes as dated rows, which is cheaper than remembering what a page said last month.

Checklist before publishing the page

  • Outcome line names the artefact and the job in one sentence
  • Inventory written in counts, formats and page counts
  • One honest exclusion: who this is not for
  • Six to eight objections answered in the buyer's own words
  • Price under the inventory, with the alternative beside it
  • Delivery, licence, update policy and refund window stated plainly
  • One button label, repeated, with nothing competing above the fold
  • Page read once on a phone at arm's length
