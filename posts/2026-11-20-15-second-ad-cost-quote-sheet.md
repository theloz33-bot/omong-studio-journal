A quote for a 15-second vertical ad can be $95 or $1,500, and both can be honest. The number that decides which one is right is not the market rate — it is the hours the work takes, multiplied by what an hour has to earn. Most one-person studios skip that second factor, quote from what a competitor charged, and find out six months later that the schedule was full and the bank account was not.

This is the arithmetic I use before sending a quote, including the part that surprised me: the second ad in a format costs about half the first, and the tenth costs less than half of that.

## The hours behind one 15-second cut

Nine shots, one concept, one voiceover line or a captioned beat, one revision round. Here is where a working day goes, from brief to delivered file.

| Stage | First cut in a format | Tenth cut in the same format | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brief and one question list | 0.5 h | 0.25 h | You already know which six answers you need |
| Script and shot list | 1.5 h | 0.5 h | The beat structure is copied, not invented |
| Stills, one per shot, with re-rolls | 3.0 h | 1.5 h | Prompts and a character sheet already exist |
| Motion, one move per clip | 2.5 h | 1.0 h | The move per shot type is decided |
| Assembly, captions, audio master | 2.5 h | 1.0 h | Presets for caption size, position, loudness |
| QA on a phone, export, delivery | 1.0 h | 0.5 h | The checklist is written down |
| Admin, invoice, file handover | 0.5 h | 0.25 h | The folder template does it |
| **Total** | **11.5 h** | **5.0 h** | |

The first column is the honest answer to "how long does a 15-second video take" — most of a day and a half, for something that plays for fifteen seconds. The second column is why the same person can quote $300 for the next one and still be profitable. The gap is not speed. It is that the reusable parts — the shot list shape, the prompts, the caption preset, the export settings — were paid for once.

Two consequences follow, and both go on the quote sheet:

- **The first job in a new format should cost more.** You are building the template. Charging the same as the tenth job means you did that work for free.
- **A second cut from the same concept is cheap.** Change the hook, keep the shots: about 1.5 hours, not 11.5. Sell it as an add-on for testing, because it is the highest-margin line you have.

## The rate floor is not the market rate

An hour has to earn your target income divided by the hours you can actually bill. A year has 2,080 working hours; a solo studio that also sells, writes and does admin bills 900 to 1,200 of them. Everything else is unpaid.

| Target income | At 900 billable hours | At 1,200 billable hours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $40,000 | $44 / h | $33 / h |
| $60,000 | $67 / h | $50 / h |
| $90,000 | $100 / h | $75 / h |

That gives a floor, not a price. Multiply the hours from the first table by your floor:

| | Hours | Floor at $44/h | Floor at $67/h |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First cut in a format | 11.5 | $506 | $771 |
| Tenth cut in the same format | 5.0 | $220 | $335 |
| Second hook, same concept | 1.5 | $66 | $101 |

Prices here are before platform fees, and the floor excludes the tools, storage and music you pay for whether or not a client shows up. That is the difference between a floor and a price: the floor is where you stop losing money, and the price is what the outcome is worth to the buyer.

Which is why a very low pilot is a marketing decision, not a pricing one. A pilot priced under the floor buys a portfolio piece and a reference; it only works if it converts, so cap how many you run a month and count them as customer acquisition rather than revenue. This studio's pilot is exactly that kind of number: a 20–30 second cut with one revision round for $95, delivered in 2–3 business days, priced as a paid test and written on the hire page as one.

## The six lines a quote needs

Six items, in this order, and the client can read the whole deal without a call.

1. **Concept and cut count.** One concept, one cut; or one concept, two hooks. Say which, because "a video" is not a scope.
2. **What is approved, and when.** Stills are approved before any motion is generated. That single sentence prevents the most expensive change a client can ask for.
3. **Revision rounds, with a definition.** One round included, defined as caption text, timing and colour; a new shot or a new concept is a rebuild, priced separately.
4. **Deliverables to spec.** 1080×1920 H.264, 30 fps, captions burned in or as SRT, audio at −14 LUFS. Specs belong in the quote, not in a later email.
5. **Usage.** Where the cut runs and for how long: organic feed only, or paid media; 30 days or perpetual. Paid amplification is a different licence and a different number.
6. **Schedule and payment.** Delivery date counted from stills approval, not from the brief. Fifty percent to start is normal for a first-time client, and it filters out the ones who were never going to pay.

## Three things that break the number

- **A claim change after stills.** The line of copy is the tightest constraint in the whole cut: every still is composed around it. Changing it late costs a full regeneration pass, roughly the 3 hour still stage plus re-assembly. Put the claim in the quote and get it in writing.
- **Late assets.** A logo delivered on day three of a two-day job does not make the job cheaper, it makes it later. State the delivery date from asset receipt and hold the line; otherwise every client learns that the deadline is soft.
- **Usage that grows quietly.** "Can we also run it as a pre-roll?" is a different licence. Quote it separately at the start, even at a small number, so the conversation happens once.
